The night before we sold Foresee Health, I pulled my copy of *The Hitchhiker’s Guide to the Galaxy* off the shelf. It fell open to page 62—the Vogon poetry scene. I read it three times while the due diligence team reviewed our audit logs.

Adams wrote the Guide as a device—a book within a book. It’s a travel guide for a galaxy that doesn’t make sense, built by beings who gave up trying to make it make sense. Last month, I printed the CMS 1500 claim form instructions. Sixty-four pages of modifier codes, cross‑references to CPT codes that map to other codes, footnotes that contradict the payer addendums. It reads like Vogon bureaucracy endorsed by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services.

We weren’t building a form filler. We were building a *Guide*.

---

### The Babel Fish Problem

In Adams’ universe, the Babel fish is a small yellow leech-like creature you stick in your ear. It translates any language instantly. Perfect communication. The catch? It proved God doesn’t exist, because if He did, He’d have made everything clear from the start.

Medical billing is a Babel fish problem turned inside out. Every term—*modifier -25*, *consultation vs. office visit*, *incident-to billing*—has six definitions depending on which billing manual you’re using, which payer reviewed last quarter, and which provider’s contract you’re under.

Our first prototype translated “office visit with procedure” into clean claim lines. It worked on the training set. Real claims? A primary care visit with a vaccine administration broke it. A specialist visit with multiple procedures billed separately broke it harder.

The Guide had an entry for this: **“Mostly harmless.”** Our annotation: *When a system works until it doesn’t, and you won’t know which until a claim gets denied for $14,000.*

---

### Don’t Panic

The Guide’s cover famously reads “**DON’T PANIC**” in large friendly letters. Our billing dashboard had the same text in the header, in the same shade of calming blue (#4A90E2). It showed when a claim’s expected reimbursement didn’t match what the payer actually paid.

Panic is the default state in medical billing. Billers panic when a claim denies for “unspecified reason.” Providers panic when their cash flow stops. We panicked when a data feed from the clearinghouse went silent at 2 AM. The friendly letters weren’t decoration—they were an instruction. Breathe. Check the logs. The answer is usually in the data, not the drama.

Last Tuesday, a feed did fail. The clearinghouse changed the EDI schema without warning. Our system flagged it, retried twice, then escalated to a human. No panic. Just: “835 file malformed. Retry in 90s.” The Guide would approve.

---

### The Answer Is 42

Deep Thought spent 7.5 million years computing the Answer to Life, the Universe, and Everything. It came back with “42.” Everyone was upset because they’d forgotten the question.

We got feature requests that felt like that. “Can the AI predict claim denial risk?” Sure. The model would spit out a number—78.2%. Billers would treat it as gospel. Providers would treat it as nonsense. The real question wasn’t “What’s the chance?” It was “What specific documentation prevents the denial, and how do we get it signed by Thursday?”

We built the prediction endpoint anyway. It returned 42 when confidence was low. It was our inside joke—a reminder that without the right question, any answer is noise.

---

### Marvin the Paranoid Android

Marvin has a brain the size of a planet, and he’s bored. He’s depressed by the futility of existence, annoyed by the trivial problems he’s asked to solve.

Our claims error detection models sometimes felt like Marvin. They could parse a 300‑line 837 claim file in seconds, extract CPT codes, modifiers, patient demographics. Then we asked them to output the exact denial reason code format the payer required, and they sighed in floating‑point precision.

The trick was giving Marvin something interesting to do. We started feeding the model edge cases—Denmark billing with six modifiers on one line, pediatric claims where the patient is the insured’s child, handwritten superbills scanned from 1995. The attention scores perked up. Less boredom, more engagement.

Even AI doesn’t want to do busywork.

---

### Towel = Preparedness

In the Guide, a towel is the most useful thing an interstellar hitchhiker can carry. It has practical uses (drying, warmth, signaling) and psychological ones—someone with a towel is *prepared*.

Our “towel” was the audit trail. Every payment—every claim line, every flag for incorrect modifier, every override by a human biller—got logged with a hash, timestamp, and actor ID. It was boring infrastructure. It was also what let us prove to an auditor that yes, the service was medically necessary, and yes, the system did catch the duplicate billing error.

Without the towel, you’re just guessing. With it, you’re prepared for the payer auditor who asks, “How do you justify this modifier -25 on an office visit?”

---

### So Long, and Thanks for All the Fish

At the end of the first book, dolphins leave Earth with that message. They figured out the planet was about to be demolished and booked it.

Sometimes I looked at the legacy medical billing software—the mainframe systems from 1985, the Access databases billers kept “just in case,” the EDI files that required fax confirmations—and thought the dolphins had the right idea. Why keep patching systems that should’ve been retired a decade ago?

Then a detection worked. A claim got flagged for missing a -59 modifier before it was submitted. A provider saved $14K on a denied procedure. The dolphins were wrong. You don’t leave when things get hard. You build a better boat.

That’s why we sold Foresee Health to PE instead of shutting it down—so someone else could keep sailing.

---

### Try It Tonight

If you’re building something that feels like Vogon bureaucracy—medical billing, payments, insurance—go read Adams again. Not for the jokes. For the framing.

1. **Build a Guide, not a manual.** Help users navigate nonsense without pretending it makes sense.
2. **Put DON’T PANIC where they’ll see it.** Errors should calm, not escalate.
3. **Log everything.** Your towel is your audit trail.
4. **Find the real question behind “42.”** Providers ask for predictions; they need clean claims.
5. **Give Marvin hard problems.** Bored models make sloppy mistakes.

The Galaxy is mostly harmless. Your code doesn’t have to be.
